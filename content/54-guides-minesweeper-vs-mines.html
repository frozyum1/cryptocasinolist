How classic Minesweeper works

You get a rectangular grid with a known number of hidden mines. Clicking a safe square reveals a number: how many mines touch that square among its eight neighbours. A blank square means zero neighbouring mines, so the game auto-opens the area around it. From those numbers you deduce which squares must be mines (flag them) and which must be safe. Hit a mine and the game ends.

A timer runs from your first click, and a counter shows mines left minus flags placed. Those two numbers are the whole scoring system, which is why the game has such a long speedrunning tradition: there is nothing to collect, unlock or buy, only a board and a clock.

Controls on desktop and mobile

The controls are the same almost everywhere. Left click opens a square. Right click places a flag on a square you believe hides a mine, and right-clicking again often cycles to a question mark. Middle click (or left and right buttons together) on an already-opened number is called chording: if you have flagged exactly as many neighbours as the number says, it opens all the remaining neighbours at once. Chording is where most of the speed in Minesweeper comes from.

On phones and tablets a tap usually opens a square and a long press flags it, or there is a toggle button that switches between dig mode and flag mode. If a mobile version keeps blowing you up by accident, switch to the toggle — it is slower but far less error-prone than long presses.

Difficulty levels and board settings

The standard difficulties most people know are beginner (9×9 with 10 mines), intermediate (16×16 with 40) and expert (30×16 with 99). Mine density rises with each step, so expert is not just a bigger board — it is a tighter one.

Two modern conveniences are worth looking for when you pick a version. Many implementations guarantee that your first click is never a mine. Some go further and guarantee a board that can be solved without guessing, which removes the coin-flip endings that older versions were famous for. A good free version states which of those it does, and lets you set a custom grid size and mine count.

Is Minesweeper a video game?

Yes, in the plain sense: it is a computer game with rules, a win state and a score. It became familiar to millions because it was bundled free with desktop operating systems for years, so a lot of people met it on an office machine rather than buying it. It is no longer preinstalled on a typical modern Windows computer, which is why most people now play it in a browser.

Minesweeper strategy that actually works

Because the puzzle gives you real information, strategy there is genuine — unlike in the casino version. The basics:

  • Open a corner or an edge first. Edge squares have fewer neighbours, so the numbers you uncover constrain fewer unknowns and are easier to resolve.
  • Count, do not guess. A number equals the mines around it. If the count of unopened neighbours equals the number, they are all mines. If the flags around it already equal the number, every other neighbour is safe.
  • Learn the common patterns. A 1-2-1 along a wall always resolves to mine-safe-mine behind the 1s and 2; a 1-2-2-1 resolves to safe-mine-mine-safe. Recognising these on sight is most of what separates a fast player from a slow one.
  • Use the global mine counter at the end. When few mines remain, the total left often decides an area that local numbers alone cannot.
  • When you must guess, guess cheaply. On boards that are not guarantee-solvable, pick the square with the lowest probability of being a mine and, where probabilities tie, the one that reveals the most information.

All of that is deduction, and none of it costs or wins money. Keep that distinction in mind for the next section.

How the casino Mines game works

Most casino versions use a 5×5 grid of twenty-five tiles. You choose how many mines to hide, usually anywhere from one to twenty-four, then place a bet. Each safe tile you open raises your multiplier; you may cash out after any pick. Open a mine and the stake is lost immediately.

There are no numbers, so there is nothing to deduce. Every unopened tile has the same chance of being a bomb at every moment. The only decisions that matter are how much you bet, how many mines you set, and when you stop. That is the whole game — and it is why Mines plays like a grid-shaped version of Crash rather than like a puzzle. We break the mechanics down further in how the Mines game works.

The maths behind a Mines round

With 25 tiles and m mines, your chance of surviving the first pick is (25 − m)/25. Survive it and the next pick is (24 − m)/24, and so on, because one safe tile has been removed from the pool. Chain those together and you get the probability of completing k safe picks.

A fair payout would be one divided by that probability. A casino instead pays slightly less, and the gap is the house edge. Lumenbet publishes a 2% house edge on Mines, so a round that has a 25% chance of succeeding pays about 0.98 ÷ 0.25 ≈ 3.92× rather than 4×. Other operators set their own edges and do not always publish them; if a Mines table does not state its edge, you cannot check how much it is taking.

Two consequences follow, and they apply to every Mines table everywhere:

  • More mines does not mean better value. Three mines with eight picks and twenty mines with one pick both carry the same percentage edge. Only volatility changes — big mine counts mean rare, large wins and long losing streaks.
  • No stopping rule beats the edge. Cashing out early, cashing out late, doubling after a loss: all of it rearranges the shape of your results, not the long-run expectation. Anyone selling a Mines "strategy" that claims otherwise is selling nothing.

Gambling can be addictive. Set a session budget you would be fine losing before you open a single tile, and read responsible gambling if play has stopped feeling optional.

Can skill transfer from one to the other?

Barely. The part of Minesweeper that makes you good — reading patterns like 1-2-1, counting flags against numbers, working edges first — depends entirely on information the casino version removes.

What does transfer is discipline: puzzle players are used to acting on known odds instead of hunches. In Mines the known odds are simple. Each additional pick carries the same percentage house edge as the first, so continuing is not a mathematically worse bet in edge terms. What changes is exposure: the amount riding on the next tile is your whole accumulated balance for that round, while the multiplier step it adds is proportionally smaller than the earlier ones. Deciding how much risk you want on the table is the only real decision you have.

There is also a trap worth naming. Because the grid looks familiar, Mines feels like a skill game to people who grew up on the puzzle. That feeling is the product working as designed. A tile you "feel good about" has exactly the same chance of hiding a bomb as the one next to it.

FAQ

Is the casino Mines game the same as Minesweeper?

No. They share a grid and bombs, nothing else. Minesweeper gives you numbered clues and can be solved by logic; casino Mines hides all information, so every unopened tile carries the same risk and the outcome is chance plus a house edge.

Can I win at Mines with Minesweeper skill?

No. There are no clues to deduce from. The only levers are stake size, mine count and when you cash out, and none of them change the expected return.

What mine count gives the best odds?

None of them, in percentage terms. Low mine counts give frequent small multipliers, high counts give rare large ones, and the house edge stays the same across settings on a given casino's table.
