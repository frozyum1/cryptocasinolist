What a mirror domain is, in general

A mirror (or alternative domain) is one website served under a second domain name. It is not a sister brand, a white-label clone or a separate company — in the normal case it is the same application, reachable by a different address. That is a common pattern across online gambling, and it is also a common pattern in phishing, which is exactly why a domain you have never heard of deserves a check rather than a login.

Operators use second domains for two main reasons:

  • Network-level blocking. Some internet providers filter gambling domains at the DNS level. A second address can route around a filter on the domain name itself. It does not route around a country restriction: if a casino does not accept players from your country, a different address will not change that, because the rule applies to the account and the operator's licence conditions, not to the URL bar.
  • Advertising and attribution. Ad networks and affiliate partners sometimes need a dedicated domain per campaign so clicks and sign-ups can be measured separately. That is why an advert can drop you on a brand name you have never seen before — and why "what is this domain" searches exist at all.

How to check a domain yourself

Do not start by logging in. Entering credentials is the one action a phishing site needs from you, so it should come last, not first.

  1. Start from an address you already trust. Open the casino's known site, log in there, and look for the alternative address inside your account, in the support chat, or in an email sent from the account you registered with. An operator that runs a second domain can confirm it to you directly; a stranger's ad cannot.
  2. Ask support on the known address. "Is domain X yours?" is a one-line question with a one-line answer. Ask it in the chat on the site you already use, not in the chat on the site you are checking.
  3. Read the domain character by character. Look-alike domains swap letters, add hyphens, or append words such as "-official", "-win", "-casino" or a country code.
  4. Never type a seed phrase or private key. No casino login needs one. A page that asks is stealing, not authenticating.
  5. Only use deposit addresses generated inside your logged-in account. An address sent to you in a chat, a comment, a DM or an email is not yours to use.
  6. Bookmark what you confirm. Paid search results and social messages are where spoofed domains get most of their traffic. A bookmark removes that whole attack surface.

One more practical note that trips people up even on genuine second domains: passkeys and browser-saved passwords are bound to the domain they were created on. A passkey or an autofill entry from one address will simply not appear on another, which feels like something is broken when nothing is. That is a browser rule, not a sign of anything in particular — good or bad — about the site you are looking at.

If a casino domain will not load

Try a different network first — mobile data instead of home Wi-Fi — then flush your DNS cache or switch to a public resolver. If the site loads on one network and not another, you are looking at provider-level filtering rather than downtime, and support on the operator's known address can tell you which addresses they run. Using a VPN to get around a country block is against the terms at most casinos, so read the terms of the site you play on before you reach for one; a sudden country mismatch is also a classic trigger for a manual review on an account with a balance in it.
